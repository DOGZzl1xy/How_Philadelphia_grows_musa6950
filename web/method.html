Method

A few LiDAR flights teach a satellite foundation model to find new buildings in the aerial photographs Philadelphia takes every spring. Street-level photographs then find the added floors that cannot be seen from above. This page describes the data, the labels, the first attempts, the model and its training, the single test that decides what the model is used for, and the look from the street.

Research workflow

1. Study area and data

The study area is the city's residential and low- to mid-density mixed-use zoning: 152.5 km² and 526,928 building lots in 46 ZIP codes. The high-density centre, industrial and special-purpose districts are excluded, and 250 m blocks dominated by high-rises are reported separately, leaving 520,903 lots. A lot is a 2016 parcel linked to the 2025 parcels; parcels that overlap are merged so that a lot split does not look like construction.

Each LiDAR flight was turned into a 0.5 m grid of height above ground with a vegetation mask (2,661 tiles). The 2022 point cloud classifies only ground, so canopy is found from multiple laser returns; the 2025 flight (18 April, leaves out) also uses the point classes. The 2018 flight is sparse outside the centre, so periods are compared on the lots covered in both.

2. LiDAR as the teacher

A building cell is a 0.5 m cell higher than 2.5 m that is not vegetation. For each lot, the building cells of two flights are compared.

  • New building: building cover rose from under 20% to over 50%.
  • Added floor (pop-up): at least 30% of the earlier roof rose by 2.5 m or more.
  • Demolition: the reverse of a new building.

In ZIP code 19146, where the rules were developed, the labels recover 96.6% of completed new-construction permits and 87.1% of upward-addition permits. In two blind reviews, 95.5% and 95.7% of the flagged changes were real.

3. First attempts

A DINOv31 model supervised by LiDAR already maps tree height from satellite images2, so the first attempt asked for building height from a single photograph.

Building height from a single photograph in ZIP codes and years the models never saw: a U-Net pretrained on ImageNet, DINOv3 frozen with a linear head, and DINOv3 fine-tuned with a DPT decoder.

The satellite-pretrained, fine-tuned model was the most accurate and the most stable on the new 2025 photographs. Change is harder than height: published detectors reach an F1 of 92–95% on benchmarks where change is dense3, about 81% where it is sparse4 and 69% in unseen cities5. Asked for the change in height, two photographs alone answered "no change" everywhere, and with the measured height added the model copied it. A score for every lot is what worked.

4. The model

One DINOv3 encoder, shared by both photographs; a decoder that also takes the measured height; one answer per building.

  • Encoder: DINOv3 ViT-L/161 (about 300 million parameters), pretrained by Meta without labels on 493 million satellite images (SAT-493M). It reads each 128 m window (512 × 512 pixels, the photographs resampled to 0.25 m) as 16 × 16-pixel patches.
  • Inputs: the photograph from a LiDAR year, the LiDAR height of that year (and its vegetation mask), and a photograph from a later spring.
  • Decoder: a DPT-style decoder6 fuses the two feature maps, their difference and the measured height.
  • Per building: features are pooled over each lot (mean and maximum, plus the share of the lot predicted to rise by 2.5 m or more). Two heads score whether the lot grew and whether the growth is an added floor; a lot that grew but not by an added floor is a new building. A height-change map is learned alongside as a helper.

6. One sealed test

Eight ZIP codes (15% of the study area) were drawn at random and sealed, and the period 2022→2025 never entered training. Everything applied to the test was fixed on validation first: each head's threshold maximises F1, and counts are calibrated by validation precision ÷ recall (0.90 for new buildings). The bar was set in advance too: a correlation of at least 0.8 between the model's and LiDAR's counts per 250 m block. The test was run once.

New buildings pass the bar (r = 0.96, 95% interval 0.67–0.98), so the model fills the years between flights. Single buildings are less reliable (about 70% precision and recall), so results are reported for blocks and neighbourhoods.

7. What it cannot see

Added floors fail the bar (r = 0.42). An added floor is nearly invisible in a photograph taken straight down, and added floors were five times rarer in the test period than in validation, so false alarms outnumber them. Added floors are therefore counted from LiDAR alone.

8. From the street

One row of houses in 2016, 2018 and 2019 (Google Street View, redrawn in two tones; the added floor is set in orange). The model says between which two photographs a house grew.

An added floor shows from the side, so the project looks at street-level photographs. Houses to check come from three hints: the aerial model's own added-floor score, building permits that describe an upward addition, and zoning permits for an addition or new development. They are checked in the 571 blocks of 250 m where the aerial model marks at least three buildings as grown, which gives 15,401 houses, and then in the rest of the study area, 8,418 more.

For each house, the Google Street View capture point nearest its lot supplies one photograph per period (before the 2018 flight, 2018–2019, 2020–2023, 2024 on), cut from the panorama towards the lot. 16,738 houses (70%) have photographs on both sides of 2018–2025. A vision-language model (Gemini 3.8 Flash; outside the 571 blocks the cheaper Gemini Decision 1) reads all the dated photographs of a house together and answers whether it became taller, was newly built on an empty lot, did not change, or cannot be judged, and between which two photographs it changed.

9. From the model to the city

  • Years between flights: the model scores the 2019 and 2020 photographs against the 2018 LiDAR, and 2023 and 2024 against 2022, on one fixed set of 261,056 lots covered by all three flights.
  • Rates: new buildings per 1,000 lots per year, mapped in 250 m blocks and block groups.
  • Transit and income: distance to the nearest rail-type stop; negative binomial models per block group with rail distance, income level and change, population change and rowhouse share.
  • Permits: matched to lots by property account, to measure how many permits became buildings and how many buildings had no permit.

10. Limitations

  • Results hold for blocks and neighbourhoods, not for single buildings.
  • The look from the street reaches only houses photographed both before 2020 and from 2024 on, and it finds added floors without counting them.
  • Added-floor trends rest on two LiDAR periods only.
  • The 2018 flight covers 64% of lots, mostly in the centre.
  • The relationships with transit and income are associations, not causes.
