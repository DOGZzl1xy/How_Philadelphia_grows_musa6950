Method

A few LiDAR flights teach a satellite foundation model to read new construction from the aerial photographs Philadelphia takes every spring. Street-level photographs then find the extra storeys that cannot be seen from above. This page describes the data, the labels, the model, its training, the single test that decides what the model may be used for, and the look from the street.

The project on one sheet: what goes in, the one test that decides, and what comes out.

1. Study area and data

The study area is the city's residential and low- to mid-density mixed-use zoning: 152.5 km² and 526,928 building lots in 46 ZIP codes. The high-density centre, industrial and special-purpose districts are excluded, and 250 m cells dominated by high-rises are reported separately, leaving 520,903 lots. A lot is a 2016 parcel linked to the 2025 parcels; parcels that overlap are merged so that a lot split does not look like construction.

Each LiDAR flight was turned into a 0.5 m grid of height above ground with a vegetation mask (2,661 tiles). The 2022 point cloud classifies only ground, so canopy is found from multiple laser returns; the 2025 flight (18 April, leaves out) also uses the point classes. The 2018 flight is sparse outside the centre, so periods are compared on the lots covered in both.

2. LiDAR as the teacher

For each lot and each pair of flights, a building cell is higher than 2.5 m and not vegetation.

  • New construction: building cover rose from under 20% to over 50%.
  • Extra storey (pop-up): at least 30% of the earlier roof rose by 2.5 m or more.
  • Demolition: the reverse of new construction.

The labels are reliable: in the pilot ZIP code they recover 96.6% of completed new-construction permits and 87.1% of upward-addition permits, and in two blind reviews 95.5% and 95.7% of the flagged changes were real.

3. The model

One DINOv3 encoder, shared by both photographs; a decoder that also takes the measured height; one answer per building.

  • Encoder: DINOv3 ViT-L/16 (about 300 million parameters), pretrained by Meta without labels on 493 million satellite images (SAT-493M). It reads each 128 m window (512 × 512 pixels at 0.25 m) as 16 × 16-pixel patches.
  • Inputs: the photograph from a LiDAR year, the LiDAR height of that year (and its vegetation mask), and a photograph from a later spring.
  • Decoder: a DPT-style decoder fuses the two feature maps, their difference and the measured height.
  • Per building: features are pooled over each lot (mean and maximum, plus the share of the lot predicted to rise by 2.5 m or more). Two heads score whether the lot grew and whether the growth is an extra storey; a lot that grew but not by an extra storey is new construction. A height-change map is learned alongside as a helper.

5. Why a foundation model

Building height from a single photograph in ZIP codes and years the models never saw: a U-Net pretrained on ImageNet, DINOv3 frozen with a linear head, and DINOv3 fine-tuned with a DPT decoder.

The satellite-pretrained, fine-tuned model was the most accurate and the most stable on the new 2025 photographs. Two simpler routes to change also failed: two photographs alone, asked for height change, collapsed to "no change"; adding the LiDAR height made the model copy it. Scoring each building directly is what worked. As a baseline for that final task, simply subtracting the two photographs (after matching their brightness) reaches an average precision of 0.06 against the model's 0.80.

6. One sealed test

Eight ZIP codes (15% of the study area) were drawn at random and sealed, and the period 2022→2025 never entered training. Everything applied to the test was fixed on validation first: each head's threshold maximises F1, and counts are calibrated by validation precision ÷ recall (0.90 for new construction). The test was run once.

New construction passes the bar set in advance (r ≥ 0.8 at 250 m), so the model may fill the years between flights. Single buildings are less reliable (about 70% precision and recall), so results are reported for blocks and neighbourhoods.

7. What it cannot see

An extra storey keeps the same roof from above; only its height changes.

Extra storeys fail the bar (r = 0.42). An added storey is nearly invisible in a photograph taken straight down, and extra storeys were five times rarer in the test period than in validation, so false alarms outnumber them. Extra storeys are therefore counted from LiDAR alone. Shadows could in principle reveal height, but in rowhouse blocks they fall on neighbouring roofs, and flight dates and times differ between years.

8. From the street

One row of houses in 2016, 2018 and 2019 (Google Street View, redrawn in two tones; the added storey is set in orange). The model says between which two photographs a house grew.

An extra storey shows from the side, so the project looks at street-level photographs. Houses to check come from three hints: the aerial model's own extra-storey score, building permits that describe an upward addition, and zoning permits for an addition or new development. They are checked in the 571 blocks of 250 m where the aerial model marks at least three buildings as grown, which gives 15,401 houses.

For each house, the capture point nearest its lot supplies one photograph per period (before the 2018 flight, 2018–2019, 2020–2023, 2024 on), cut from the panorama towards the lot. 11,139 houses (72%) have photographs on both sides of 2018–2025. A vision-language model (Gemini) reads the two to five dated photographs of a house together and answers whether it became taller, was newly built on an empty lot, did not change, or cannot be judged, and between which two photographs it changed. The prompt, the choice of photographs and the list of hints were settled in one ZIP code and then frozen.

9. From the model to the city

  • Years between flights: the model scores the 2019 and 2020 photographs against the 2018 LiDAR, and 2023 and 2024 against 2022, on one fixed set of 261,056 lots so that a change in coverage cannot pass for a trend.
  • Rates: new buildings per 1,000 lots per year, mapped in 250 m cells and block groups.
  • Transit and income: distance to the nearest rail-type stop; negative binomial models per block group with rail distance, income level and change, population change and rowhouse share.
  • Permits: matched to lots by property account, to measure how many permits became buildings and how many buildings had no permit.

10. Limitations

  • Results hold for blocks and neighbourhoods, not for single buildings.
  • The look from the street reaches only houses photographed both around 2018 and after 2024, and it finds extra storeys without counting them.
  • Extra-storey trends rest on two LiDAR periods only.
  • The 2018 flight covers 64% of lots, mostly in the centre.
  • The relationships with transit and income are associations, not causes.
